# Anti-patterns

Read this before generating. These are not "bad words": most were good names once. They are **exhausted defaults**: the options a language model (or a rushed human) reaches for first, which means the market is already wallpapered with them. Reaching for them is not a style crime, it's a distinctiveness failure, and distinctiveness is the one property a name cannot survive without.

## The exhausted lexicon

Kill on sight unless a written justification survives review (see "When an exhausted word is still right" below):

**Space & light**: Nova, Nebula, Orbit, Astra, Astral, Stellar, Cosmos, Comet, Eclipse, Lumen, Lumina, Lux, Halo, Aura, Beacon, Prism, Solstice, Zenith, Aether/Ether, Nimbus

**Peaks & geometry**: Apex, Vertex, Summit, Pinnacle, Meridian, Axis, Axiom, Vector, Delta, Sigma, Element, Core, Arc

**Motion & energy**: Flux, Pulse, Surge, Spark, Ember, Ignite, Kinetic, Momentum, Velocity, Drift, Shift, Leap, Bolt

**Craft & mystique**: Forge, Loom, Anvil, Quill, Cipher, Rune, Arcane, Alchemy, Oracle (taken anyway), Sage, Scribe

**Nature-calm**: Haven, Harbor, Bloom, Grove, Willow, Fern, Moss, Ridge, Vale, Solace

**Connection & trust**: Nexus, Synergy, Catalyst, Vault, Anchor, Bridge, Relay, Atlas, Sentinel, Aegis, Veritas, Kairos

**AI-era declension**: Neural-, Cogn-, Synth-, Infer-, and the -ix / -ex / -tex endings that produce Neuralift, Cognitex, Inferix, Synthetix. The 2024-2026 generator graveyard; every name-generator tool reaches for them first.

**Greek/Latin startup declension**: anything ending in -us, -um, -ia, -os chosen *because it sounds Latin*, not because the root means something (produces pharma or crypto vibes: Novum, Alteus, Veridia, Kryptos…)

This list is illustrative, not exhaustive. The test generalizes: *if the word feels instantly "startup-appropriate", that feeling is the evidence that ten thousand founders had it before you.*

## Pattern traps

- **-ly suffix** (Bitly, Grammarly era): reads as 2012. Same for **-ify** (forever in Spotify/Shopify's shadow) and **-io / -eo** bolted on to rescue a taken .com.
- **Dropped vowels** (Flickr, Tumblr era): a permanent spelling tax for a dated signal.
- **"AI" bolted on**: SomethingAI names the technology, not the brand, and dates itself the way "Cyber-" and "e-" did. There is no fundraising premium to buy back the cost: in a matched sample of 200 YC AI companies (Technical.ly, August 2026), the half without "AI" in the name raised at least as much as the half with it. The test that survives: will the word still describe the company in ten years? Lead with the problem solved, not the technology behind it. **The `.ai` TLD is a different decision**: a namespace and an audience signal, chosen at domain time, not a defect in the word. An empty name on `.ai` is usually more defensible than a name that explains the AI; a `.ai` domain never licenses "AI" inside the name.
- **Descriptive of the technology, not of the brand**: n8n, tRPC, LangChain, Character.AI. Loved tools, filename names: the first two are unpronounceable by design, the third names a 2022 architecture, the fourth uses the TLD as a crutch. Fine for a library that will only ever be typed; fatal for anything that must be said.
- **Mushy portmanteaus**: blends where the seam still shows — `Innova|tech`, `Market|ly`, `Financ|io`. A blend earns its place only when it reads as one natural word (Pinterest, Netflix). If you can see the weld, cut it.
- **Random CVCV tech-strings**: the `Zy-lo`, `Ve-xo`, `Qo-rix` shape. Pronounceable, ownable, and completely hollow: coined names work when built from meaningful morphemes or deliberate sound symbolism, not from a syllable shaker.
- **Internal capitals**: a name that needs its capitals to be read (DeFiLlama, eBay-style camel case, anything with a category acronym welded in) gets respelled by everyone who types it, and the logo has to teach the case for the rest of the brand's life. Same family: **the category word in the name** (DeFi-, -Finance, -Coaching, -Labs) fixes the activity and dates with it; "AI bolted on" above is one instance of a general defect.
- **Misspelling without a reason**: Lyft earns its Y (distinct from the verb, trademark-able). Kwik does not.
- **Domain leakage**: letting "get", "use", "try", "hq", "app" migrate from the domain into the name itself.
- **Two random nouns**: arbitrary pairings can be excellent (that's how arbitrary marks work), but only when the pairing is *chosen* for texture, sound, and image. "Blue Otter Labs" assembled by dice roll has the shape of a name and none of the intent.

## Process anti-patterns

These kill more projects than bad words do:

- **Presenting the first ten ideas.** The obvious layer is what every competitor also generated. Quantity first is not a ritual; it is how you get past the shared layer.
- **Letting domain availability choose the name.** Availability filters; strategy decides. A weaker name with a free .com is still a weaker name (and the .com problem has workarounds; see `verification.md`).
- **Choosing by preference poll.** "Which do you like?" reliably selects the safest, most generic option, because distinctive names polarize before they settle. iPad and Wii were both publicly mocked at announcement; both outlived the mockery. Present names argued against the brief, not as a beauty contest.
- **Naming by committee compromise.** Merging two camps' favorites produces a name nobody chose and nothing defends.
- **The name that needs a paragraph.** If the rationale must travel with the name for it to work, the name doesn't work. Rationale is for the decision meeting, not the customer.

## When an exhausted word is still right

The lexicon above is a checkpoint, not a law. An exhausted word passes when the *context flips it*: a specific, argued reason this word is not wallpaper **here**: a heritage constraint from the client, an ironic or literal read in the category (a moving company called Atlas is doing something a fintech called Atlas is not), or a combination that renews it. The rule is procedural: an exhausted word never ships without its justification written into the shortlist rationale. If the justification reads thin, it is.

A third case, distinct from the two above: **the category is new and the word is free.** An exhausted word is exhausted in the categories that used it. When a category has just appeared and nobody has named it yet, the first product to take a clean real word owns it there, even if the word is wallpaper elsewhere: Astro (2021, no spatial framework existed), Neon (2021, no serverless Postgres brand existed), Cursor (2023, no AI editor had taken the obvious object). The test is procedural: (1) the category has no incumbent with a name of the same family, and (2) the word is available in the TLD and the trademark class that matter. Both must be true and both must be written into the rationale. This case expires fast: eighteen months after the first mover, the same word is wallpaper again (see the -AI wave and the Cursor-era surf names in `corpus.md` §20).
