# Corpus

Real names, filed by type, with what each one buys and what it costs. Read during generation (step 3) to calibrate what each type looks like when it works, and during evaluation (step 4) to compare a candidate against real names of the same type. Not a list to copy from: every name here is taken. The value is in the two lines under each name.

Format per entry: **Name** (what it names). Works: why. Costs: the tax it pays. Facts are limited to what is documented; where an origin story is folklore it is marked as such.

## How to use this file

- During generation, when a territory produces only one type of name, open the matching section and read five entries: the range within a type is the point.
- During evaluation, find the closest real name to the candidate and ask whether the candidate pays the same tax with the same budget. A tiny tool cannot afford Apple's tax.
- Never propose a name from this file. Propose a name that would sit in one of these sections without embarrassment.

## 1. Descriptive

**Booking.com** (travel). Works: instant comprehension, global SEO gift in the 2000s. Costs: the mark is the domain; the word itself is unprotectable (the company lost the generic-term argument in several jurisdictions, and won in the US only as "booking.com", not "booking").
**Whole Foods** (grocery). Works: the descriptor doubles as a promise. Costs: locks the positioning; "whole" means nothing outside food.
**Dollar Shave Club** (razors). Works: price, product, model in three words; a launch-video name. Costs: the dollar stopped being true within years; the name outlived its claim.
**The Weather Channel** (media). Works: zero explanation. Costs: zero ownership; the brand lives in the logo and the anchors.
**Leboncoin** (French classifieds). Works: colloquial, warm, says what it is ("the good spot"). Costs: French only; unexportable.
**Cars.com / Hotels.com** (marketplaces). Works: category ownership through the domain. Costs: interchangeable with any competitor that buys the next generic domain; brand equity is rented from the TLD.

Lesson: descriptive names work when the product is the category (or wants to be) and the budget is in distribution, not in brand.

## 2. Suggestive

**Slack** (chat). Works: a real word, a felt quality (looseness, breathing room), one syllable, verbable. Costs: crowded generic word; the company later leaned on "Slack Technologies" for trademark reach. Folklore: the "Searchable Log of All Conversation and Knowledge" backronym came after the name.
**Amazon** (everything). Works: scale, alphabetical advantage in 1995 directories, no link to books so it survived the pivot. Costs: none visible today; the cost was the 1995 confidence to call a bookstore after a river.
**Notion** (docs). Works: abstract but warm; suggests thought without saying "notes". Costs: common word, weak search uniqueness; the .so domain was the price.
**Stripe** (payments). Works: a physical, textured word (magnetic stripe) that never says "payments". Costs: common word; ownable only because the company got big fast.
**Linear** (issue tracking). Works: promises the feeling (straight, unbroken flow) in the category's own register. Costs: adjective, weak on its own; needs the .app domain and the product to hold it.
**Zoom** (video). Works: verb, speed, sound. Costs: the word was already on the shelf (Zoom Telephonics, Zoom the kids' TV show); trademark friction was inevitable.
**Calm** and **Headspace** (meditation). Works: both name the promised state. Costs: Calm is unprotectable as a word; Headspace is a compound that reads as one.
**Superhuman** (email). Works: names the user's feeling, not the product; big claim, big memorability. Costs: an adjective with a Nietzsche and comic-book tail; if the product is merely fine, the name mocks it.
**Ramp** (fintech). Works: motion, upward, one syllable; the category's aggression in a calm word. Costs: generic word, SEO tax.
**Mercury** (fintech). Works: speed, liquidity, planet, metal; a metaphor with four readings. Costs: hundreds of Mercurys already exist (insurance, cars, records); heavy trademark scoping.
**Chime** and **Starling** (neobanks). Works: small, friendly, sonic, nothing bank-like. Costs: in a category full of birds and bells, both are already crowded.
**Wise** (ex-TransferWise). Works: the descriptive half was dropped once the product outgrew transfers; a one-word adjective the customer would like to be. Costs: unprotectable alone; the rename was an expensive bet on brand scale.
**Vinted** (second-hand fashion). Works: "vintage" clipped into a verb-shaped past participle; sounds like a state ("I got vinted"). Costs: coined, so it explained nothing at launch.

## 3. Metaphor / symbol

**Nike** (sportswear). Works: the goddess of victory, two syllables, a built-in image. Costs: mispronounced for a decade ("nyke"); the company ran ads to fix it.
**Puma**, **Jaguar**, **Mustang** (speed brands). Works: the animal carries the promise. Costs: exhausted family; new entrants get "one more cat".
**Red Bull** (energy drink). Works: force, colour, a printable symbol; Krating Daeng translated, so the metaphor came with the product. Costs: aggressive; unusable outside energy and extreme sports, which is the whole strategy.
**Dove** (soap). Works: softness and peace in one bird. Costs: two Doves coexist (Unilever soap, Mars chocolate) because the classes never touched; would be impossible to start today.
**Robinhood** (trading). Works: the story is the positioning (take from the rich). Costs: the story became a liability during the 2021 restrictions; a metaphor that promises virtue is a metaphor that can be turned.
**Acorns** (micro-investing). Works: small thing grows into big thing, told in one word. Costs: cute; hard to stretch upmarket.
**Docker** (containers). Works: the metaphor and the technology are the same word; the logo drew itself. Costs: shipping-container names flooded the category after it.
**Kubernetes** (orchestration). Works: Greek helmsman, continues Docker's nautical story. Costs: the worst spelling and pronunciation tax in the industry; the community invented "k8s" as a workaround. A name that needs an abbreviation to be typed has paid too much.
**Mistral** (AI, France). Works: a French wind, fast and cold, from the founders' geography; readable in every language. Costs: exhausted-adjacent (weather names), and Mistral was already a font, a helicopter and a champagne.
**Ornikar** (French driving school). Works: "or, ni, car", the conjunction mnemonic every French child learns; the name is a joke the whole market gets. Costs: untranslatable, unpronounceable for non-French; fine, the market is France.
**En Voiture Simone** (French driving school). Works: an idiom ("let's go") already in the customer's mouth. Costs: three words, no domain purity, and the idiom's origin (a 1930s racing driver) is unknown to most, so it reads as playful only.

## 4. Arbitrary

**Apple** (computers). Works: the concrete, sensory, friendly word in a category of acronyms in 1976. Costs: Apple Corps (the Beatles) litigation from 1978 to 2007; an arbitrary mark is only free in your class.
**Orange** (telecom). Works: colour, fruit, warmth; as far from "Telecom" as possible. Costs: a colour is hard to own; the identity had to do all the work.
**Camel** (cigarettes). Works: exotic, textured, drawable. Costs: none that the category noticed.
**Virgin** (everything). Works: audacity as a brand; the word itself is the attitude. Costs: unusable in several cultures without a smile.
**Lemonade** (insurance). Works: the least insurance-like word available; sweet, cheap, summer. Costs: needs the product to be actually friendlier, or the contrast becomes irony.
**Plaid** (fintech infrastructure). Works: a texture (woven lines, connections) for an invisible product. Costs: common noun, strong SEO tax, and the pattern is culturally loaded (Scotland, lumberjacks).
**Toast** (restaurant software). Works: warm, food-adjacent without saying restaurant; verbable. Costs: shares the shelf with every bakery.
**Casper** (mattresses). Works: a friendly first name for a friendly ghost; sleep by association. Costs: another Casper exists in every category.
**Alan** and **Lydia** (French insurance and payments). Works: a first name makes a cold category human; both were bold in France in the mid-2010s. Costs: no ownability outside the class, and the name is a person in every conversation ("send it to Lydia").
**Free** (French telecom). Works: the boldest arbitrary word in French telecom; a promise and a provocation. Costs: unprotectable as a word; the company owns it through the identity and 20 years of pricing.
**Hugging Face** (AI). Works: an emoji as a name; instantly memorable, disarming. Costs: enterprise buyers had to say it in board meetings; the company kept it anyway, which became part of the brand.

## 5. Coined: morpheme-built

**Accenture** (consulting). Works: "accent on the future", forced by arbitration in 2001 and produced by an internal contest; it aged better than expected because the sound is smooth. Costs: corporate blandness, exactly the pharma texture the anti-patterns list warns about.
**Verizon** (telecom). Works: veritas + horizon; sayable, ownable, huge. Costs: means nothing until advertising made it mean something; a budget name.
**Novartis** (pharma). Works: novae artes, respectable roots. Costs: indistinguishable from twenty other pharma names built the same way.
**Spotify** (music). Works: spot + identify (the founders' account) or a misheard word (their other account); the -ify was fresh in 2006. Costs: it invented the -ify wave that the anti-patterns list now kills.
**Instagram** (photos). Works: instant + telegram; the seam is invisible because both halves are familiar. Costs: none; the rare blend that reads as one word.
**Qonto** (French neobank). Works: Q is rare and ownable; sounds like "compte" without being it. Costs: has to be spelled on the phone once.
**Klarna** (payments). Works: from Swedish "klar" (clear, ready); Nordic texture exported. Costs: reads as coined everywhere except Sweden.
**Vaseline** (petroleum jelly). Works: Wasser + elaion (water + oil), 1870s morpheme building. Costs: became generic in some markets.
**Sony** (electronics). Works: sonus + "sonny", chosen in 1958 to be sayable in every language and written in Latin letters so it would not read as Japanese. Costs: none; the reference case for a global empty vessel.

## 6. Coined: empty vessel

**Kodak** (film). Works: George Eastman designed it for the letter K and for opening and closing on a plosive; the first fully engineered name. Costs: none while the company lasted; the name outlived the business.
**Exxon** (oil). Works: chosen in 1972 after a multi-year search for a word that meant nothing in any language; the double X was the point. Costs: reportedly one of the most expensive naming exercises in history, which only an oil major could pay.
**Rolex** (watches). Works: Hans Wilsdorf wanted a word pronounceable in every language and short enough for a dial. Costs: none.
**Häagen-Dazs** (ice cream). Works: fake Danish invented in the Bronx for texture and the umlaut; the map of Denmark was on the early packaging. Costs: unpronounceable on purpose; the tax is the brand.
**Google** (search). Works: misspelling of googol, a word for scale; the misspelling made it ownable. Costs: none, but note it started as "BackRub".
**Zynga** (games). Works: the founder's dog; empty, sayable, ownable. Costs: nothing to say about it except the dog.
**Twilio** (communications API). Works: pure sound, easy to say, dev-friendly. Costs: explains nothing; the docs had to.
**Etsy** (marketplace). Works: two syllables, no meaning, warm sound; the founder said he wanted a word from nowhere. Costs: none.

## 7. Compound

**Facebook** (social). Works: a real object (the college facebook) that already existed; descriptive at birth, arbitrary once the product outgrew it. Costs: none, thanks to scale.
**YouTube** (video). Works: you + tube (TV); playful, sayable. Costs: the "tube" half is dead technology; nobody notices.
**Salesforce** (CRM). Works: two words, one meaning, 1999 clarity. Costs: the company has not sold to sales forces only for fifteen years; a compound that names today's customer ages.
**Dropbox** (files). Works: the metaphor is the interface. Costs: launched at getdropbox.com because dropbox.com was taken; bought it later. The name was right; the domain lagged.
**Basecamp** (project management). Works: the metaphor of the expedition, a place to return to. Costs: shared with hundreds of outdoor brands.
**Mailchimp** (email). Works: the monkey made a boring category friendly; the second half is pure personality. Costs: unserious in enterprise rooms; the company chose that.
**Substack** (newsletters). Works: sub(scription) + stack; reads as a tech word for a media product. Costs: cold; the name is infrastructure, the product is writers.
**Cloudflare** (infrastructure). Works: the second word makes the first one energetic. Costs: "cloud" dates it; already survived.
**Back Market** (refurbished, France). Works: the second-hand market said sideways; exportable. Costs: reads as "backstreet" to some.
**Payfit** and **Aircall** (French SaaS). Works: clear, exportable, category-signalling. Costs: the noun+noun SaaS sameness the anti-patterns list names; both are fine, neither is distinctive.
**Blue Bottle** (coffee). Works: a real 17th-century Vienna coffeehouse name; colour + object, drawable. Costs: shared with a fly.

## 8. Blend / portmanteau

**Pinterest** (pin + interest). Works: invisible seam; reads as one word. Costs: none; the reference blend.
**Netflix** (net + flicks). Works: the seam shows slightly and it still won; "net" dated, nobody cares. Costs: none today.
**Microsoft** (microcomputer + software). Works: 1975 clarity. Costs: corporate; would be killed by the anti-patterns list today, and rightly.
**Groupon** (group + coupon). Works: the model in one word. Costs: named the mechanism, and when the mechanism went out of fashion the name went with it.
**Airbnb** (air bed and breakfast). Works: the origin story is the name. Costs: unpronounceable for years ("air-b-n-b"); worked because the product was the only one.
**Doctolib** (docteur + libre, France). Works: functional clarity for a market that needed to trust it. Costs: the seam is visible ("docto-lib"); acceptable because the audience is patients, not brand people.
**Spendesk** (spend + desk). Works: clear for finance teams. Costs: visible weld, exactly the shape the anti-patterns list calls mushy; survives on product, not name.
**Supabase** (super + database). Works: the developer audience forgives a visible seam if the joke lands. Costs: the joke is one-note.
**Compaq** (compatibility + quality). Works: 1982 blend with an engineered misspelling. Costs: gone with the company.
**Duolingo** (duo + lingo). Works: playful, sayable everywhere. Costs: none.

## 9. Eponymous

**Chanel**, **Ferrari**, **Dyson**, **Bose** (craft brands). Works: the person is the guarantee. Costs: the founder's death, scandal or exit becomes the brand's.
**Tesla** (cars). Works: honorific eponymy, borrowing an inventor's mythology; the name was bought from a previous owner for a reported $75,000 in 2004. Costs: none the company noticed.
**McKinsey**, **Deloitte** (services). Works: heritage as trust. Costs: the surname era in consulting is closed; a new firm called "Dupont" reads as small.
**Warby Parker** (glasses). Works: two names lifted from Kerouac's journals, so it sounds like an old firm without being one. Costs: the trick only works once per category.
**Craigslist** (classifieds). Works: one person's honesty as the brand. Costs: forever 1995.
**Adidas** (Adi Dassler). Works: a nickname and a surname clipped into a coined-looking word. Costs: none; folklore backronyms followed.
**Devialet** (French audio). Works: an 18th-century engineer's surname (Vialet) with a particle, fake heritage that reads as real. Costs: pronunciation abroad.
**shadcn** (UI library). Works: a handle as a name; the dev audience knows the person. Costs: unpronounceable, unsearchable in speech, and the person is the project.

## 10. Geographic

**Patagonia** (outdoor). Works: the place is the promise (wild, far, cold). Costs: none; but a geographic name for a product that is not from there is harder to register today.
**Cisco** (networking). Works: San Francisco clipped; the bridge is the logo. Costs: none.
**Adobe** (software). Works: the creek behind the founder's house; a texture word by accident. Costs: none.
**Nokia** (telecom). Works: the river and town; a place that stopped meaning anything but the company. Costs: none.
**Evian** (water). Works: the source is the product; unarguable. Costs: only works when it is true.
**Hollister** (clothing). Works: a fake 1922 California surf town invented in 2000; heritage as fiction. Costs: a real Hollister, California exists; the fiction is now a joke.
**Amazon** again: geographic and suggestive at once, which is why it stretched.

## 11. Borrowed / foreign

**Uber** (rides). Works: German "über", above; a superlative as a company. Costs: none linguistically; the meaning matched the attitude too well.
**Lego** (toys). Works: "leg godt", play well in Danish; the company later noticed it is also Latin for "I assemble". Costs: none.
**Volvo** (cars). Works: Latin "I roll". Costs: none.
**Audi** (cars). Works: Latin translation of the founder's surname Horch ("listen"), forced by a trademark loss. Costs: none; the constraint produced the name.
**Nintendo** (games). Works: "leave luck to heaven", a phrase nobody outside Japan reads; empty vessel abroad, meaningful at home. Costs: none.
**Asana** (work software). Works: Sanskrit posture; calm imported into a stressful category. Costs: yoga studios own the word everywhere.
**Kagi** (search). Works: Japanese "key"; short, unusual, ownable. Costs: explains nothing.
**Vite** (build tool). Works: French "fast"; the promise in a word the audience half-knows. Costs: pronounced "vight" by half the users; the docs carry a pronunciation note.
**Strava** (fitness). Works: Swedish "sträva", to strive; energetic sound. Costs: none.
**Ubuntu** (Linux). Works: Nguni "humanity towards others"; the meaning is the project's ethic. Costs: pronunciation tax, accepted.
**Pennylane** (French accounting). Works: a Beatles song lifted whole; warmth for a cold category. Costs: an English reference for a French market; the trademark risk with the song title was manageable because titles are weakly protected.
**Vespa** (scooter). Works: Italian "wasp"; the sound and shape of the machine. Costs: none.

## 12. Experiential

**Safari** (browser). Works: names the act of browsing as exploration; the compass logo followed. Costs: crowded metaphor (Explorer, Navigator were already there).
**Tinder**, **Bumble**, **Hinge** (dating). Works: each names a moment of the experience (spark, buzz, joint). Costs: the category exhausted the mechanism-as-name trick in five years.
**Waze** (navigation). Works: "ways" respelled, verb-ish, sayable. Costs: the misspelling reads as 2008 (and it is).
**Kindle** (e-reader). Works: a verb for lighting a fire, chosen to mean "start reading"; nothing about screens. Costs: none.
**Twitch** (streaming). Works: the gamer's reflex as a name; energetic, short. Costs: the word has a medical meaning nobody minds.
**BlaBlaCar** (carpooling). Works: onomatopoeia of chatting in a car; a name that is the experience. Costs: silly on purpose; the company grew into it.
**Snap** (Snapchat). Works: the verb is the product. Costs: the company had to drop "chat" to stretch.
**Discord** (chat). Works: an ironic experiential name, chaos as a promise of freedom; the audience got the joke. Costs: enterprise never will.

## 13. Acronym / initialism

**IBM**, **BMW**, **HSBC**, **SAP** (giants). Works: only because the full names came first and the money came later. Costs: meaningless at birth; do not start here.
**KFC** (ex-Kentucky Fried Chicken). Works: the rename in 1991 dropped "fried" and the geography; the acronym was already in the customer's mouth. Costs: an acronym that stands for a name you no longer want to say.
**ASOS** (As Seen On Screen). Works: the original meaning died, the sound stayed. Costs: none; the rare acronym that became a word.
**IKEA** (initials + farm + village). Works: reads as a word, not an acronym; the founder's initials plus his origin. Costs: none.
**DHL**, **UPS**, **CVS**: inherited, functional, forgettable. Use as evidence that acronyms are what you get when nobody decided.
**LVMH**: the only acceptable new-ish acronym pattern, a holding that must never outshine its brands.

## 14. Domain-native

**bit.ly** (2008). Works: the first widely copied domain hack; the .ly was the name. Costs: Libya's ccTLD; the registry later seized .ly domains it found objectionable, and bitly moved its brand to bitly.com.
**del.icio.us** (2003). Works: the founding example; playful, memorable. Costs: impossible to say aloud; the company later bought delicious.com and dropped the hack.
**last.fm** (2002). Works: the TLD names the medium. Costs: Micronesia's ccTLD; fine, it lasted.
**youtu.be**, **goo.gl**, **t.co**: shorteners, not names; useful only as evidence that the hack works for links nobody says aloud.
**notion.so**, **linear.app**, **arc.net**, **cal.com**: the name is the left part; the TLD is the fallback for a taken .com, chosen to read as a phrase (Notion, so; Linear app). Works when the left part is the whole brand and the TLD is nearly silent.
**bun.sh**, **deno.land**, **remix.run**, **astro.build**, **fly.io**, **val.town**, **bolt.new**: developer tools where the domain is a two-word phrase and the phrase is the pitch. Works: typed, never said; the TLD carries the category or the verb. Costs: each one is a bet on a young TLD's registry.
**obsidian.md**: the TLD is the file format the product uses. The best kind of hack: the domain says what the product does without a word. Costs: Moldova's ccTLD.
**read.cv**, **posts.cv**: a series built on one TLD (grammar 2 in `generation.md` §9). Costs: Cape Verde's ccTLD; the brand is hostage to a registry.
**are.na**, **lu.ma**: the TLD finishes the word (the "TLD finishes a word" shape in `generation.md` §8). Works: memorable, weird. Costs: the dot falls inside the word; both fail the radio test and both accepted it.
**early.tools**, **founders.menu**, **tasteful.haus**, **monkmode.me**, **sir.golf**, **vibi.ng** (Julian Paul): indie products where every name is a phrase completed by a TLD. Works: cheap, available, self-describing. Costs: none of them could become a company without a rename, which is the trade.
**Tumblr**, **Flickr**, **Grindr** are not domain hacks; they are dropped vowels to rescue a domain. Different tax, same era, listed here to keep the two apart.

## 15. Renames that prove the empty vessel

The company changed its name, kept its product, and the new name carried nothing at birth.

- **BackRub → Google** (1997). A description of the algorithm replaced by a word for scale.
- **Cadabra → Amazon** (1995). Cadabra sounded like "cadaver" on the phone; the first documented radio-test failure in tech.
- **AuctionWeb → eBay** (1997). Echo Bay, the founder's consulting name, shortened because echobay.com was taken.
- **Blue Ribbon Sports → Nike** (1971). A distributor's name replaced by a goddess.
- **Jerry and David's Guide to the World Wide Web → Yahoo** (1994). The description could not be a brand.
- **Odeo → Twitter** (2006). Originally "twttr" in the dropped-vowel fashion; the vowels came back.
- **Burbn → Instagram** (2010). A bourbon-themed check-in app renamed for the feature that worked.
- **Picaboo → Snapchat** (2011). Forced by a trademark conflict; the forced name was better.
- **Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo → Sony** (1958). Unsayable abroad, so they built a word.
- **Lucky-Goldstar → LG** (1995). A merger name reduced to initials, then filled with "Life's Good".
- **Research In Motion → BlackBerry** (2013). The product had eaten the company; the name followed.
- **TransferWise → Wise** (2021). The descriptor dropped when the product outgrew it.
- **Vente-privée → Veepee** (2019). A French descriptive name rewritten as its own phonetic nickname for export.
- **Andersen Consulting → Accenture** (2001). Forced by arbitration; the coined name looked corporate and aged fine.

Lesson for SKILL.md step 7: the first name is rarely the last; recommend names that can be dropped without losing the product, and say when a candidate cannot.

## 16. Renames that had to be undone

- **Royal Mail → Consignia → Royal Mail** (2001-2002). A coined name nobody asked for; reversed in fifteen months after public ridicule. The reference case for "the name that needs a paragraph".
- **Tribune Publishing → tronc → Tribune Publishing** (2016-2018). Lowercase, "tribune online content"; reversed.
- **Netflix DVD → Qwikster** (2011). Announced, mocked, cancelled in three weeks. A sub-brand nobody wanted, with a Q-w-k spelling tax.
- **RadioShack → The Shack** (2009). A nickname promoted to a brand; quietly dropped.
- **HBO Max → Max → HBO Max** (2023-2025). Dropped the letters that carried the equity, then put them back.
- **Twitter → X** (2023). The verb (tweet), the bird and the word thrown away for a letter that cannot be searched, trademarked cleanly or said without context. The most expensive equity write-off in naming.
- **Isis (mobile wallet) → Softcard** (2014). External event: a name that was fine in 2010 became unsayable in 2014. Lesson: check the news cycle at shortlist time, and prefer names with no proper-noun homonym.
- **Ayds** (diet candy, 1980s). Killed by a homophone the company could not have predicted in the 1930s and could not survive in the 1980s.

## 17. Mocked at launch, normal a year later

**iPad** (2010, period jokes for a week), **Wii** (2006, "we" jokes for a month), **Alphabet** (2015), **Meta** (2021), **Google** (1998, "silly"), **Yahoo** (1994), **Twitter** (2006, "trivial"), **Slack** (2013, "sounds lazy"). All distinctive, all polarised on contact, all normalised. Use as the evidence base for the evaluation rule: a spelling failure is a kill signal; "people laughed" is not.

## 18. Myths to stop repeating

- **Chevrolet Nova in Latin America.** The "no va" story is false: the Nova sold normally in Mexico and Venezuela; "nova" and "no va" are pronounced differently in Spanish. Cite Pajero, Fitta, Mist and Vicks (in `evaluation.md`) instead; those are documented.
- **Coca-Cola as "bite the wax tadpole" in China.** Shopkeepers' unofficial transliterations, not the company's; the official name was chosen carefully. Do not use it as evidence of corporate carelessness.
- **Backronyms presented as origins.** Slack, Adidas, Yahoo, Cobol jokes: the acronym came after the name in every case. If a user asks "what does the name stand for", the honest answer for most good names is "nothing, and that is the point".

## 19. Dev and design tools, read together

The category this skill is asked about most often. Twenty names, one line each, ordered from most to least ownable.

**Figma** (coined, figurative), **Vercel** (coined, empty), **Twilio** (empty), **Kagi** (borrowed), **Zed** (letter, ownable by luck), **Warp** (exhausted lexicon, works through category ownership), **Raycast** (compound, technical texture), **Framer** (agentive noun, descriptive-adjacent), **Webflow** (compound, descriptive), **Rive** (French "riverbank", borrowed, weak search), **Spline** (math term, descriptive for insiders), **Lottie** (eponymous, Lotte Reiniger, animation pioneer), **Procreate** (verb, risky double meaning, accepted), **Affinity** (exhausted-adjacent, survives on suite naming), **Canva** (canvas clipped; coined by subtraction), **Penpot** (compound, visible weld), **Dribbble** (deliberate misspelling with a reason: distinct from the verb), **Behance** (coined, "be" + "enhance", weld visible), **Mobbin** (verb clipped, dated -in), **Cursor** (descriptive, worked because the category had no brand yet; the tax comes when a competitor wants the same word).

The pattern: the most ownable names in this category are the emptiest (Figma, Vercel, Twilio), and the audience accepts explanation-free names because it reads docs. The least ownable are descriptive (Cursor, Webflow) and survived on being first. Recommend accordingly: for a tool aimed at builders, weight distinctiveness over comprehension.

## 20. 2018-2026: the tools a builder meets today

Read with sections 14 and 19. Grouped by the four mechanisms that recur in this period, which cover most tool names better than the 14-type taxonomy does; the taxonomy stays the reference for companies and consumer brands. Format: Name (what, year). Works. Costs.

### 20a. Real word, displaced

**Linear** (issue tracking, 2019). Works: the product's promise in the category's own register; calm. Costs: adjective, unprotectable alone, lives on linear.app.
**Cursor** (AI editor, 2023). Works: the object under the hand; the word ate the category in a year. Costs: descriptive; every competitor can say "cursor" in copy; the first-mover window is the whole moat.
**Clay** (GTM data, 2017 as a spreadsheet, relaunched 2022). Works: one syllable, matter you shape. Costs: hundreds of Clays; SEO tax paid in full.
**Warp** (terminal, 2020). Works: speed and sci-fi in one word. Costs: exhausted lexicon (motion); passed on the new-category rule, expires when the next terminal wants it.
**Neon** (serverless Postgres, 2021). Works: short, electric, visible at night. Costs: exhausted lexicon (light); passed on the new-category rule, and Neon is a brand in twenty other classes.
**Ramp** (fintech, 2019). Works: one syllable, upward. Costs: generic word; shares the shelf with every "on-ramp" product.

### 20e. Names that promised a relationship the product did not keep

**Friend** (pendant, 2024), **Rabbit** (r1, 2024), **Humane** (Ai Pin, 2023). The name sets an emotional contract (companionship, a pet, humanity) that the product then has to honour on day one. When it does not, the name becomes the headline. Rule, now in the `evaluation.md` kill list: a name that promises a feeling is only allowed when the product delivers that feeling in the first minute of use; otherwise choose a name that promises less.

### 20f. The anti-top, kept as evidence

- **Anything-AI** (OnDeck AI, dScribe AI, Certus AI): self-dating; see `anti-patterns.md`.
- **Neuralift, Cognitex, Inferix, Synthetix**: the 2024-2026 generator graveyard, listed under "AI-era variants" in the exhausted lexicon.
- **n8n, tRPC, Dify, LangChain**: loved tools, filename names; descriptive of the technology, not of the brand.
- **Character.AI**: the .AI as crutch.
- **ChatGPT**: industrial descriptive; won by force, not by name. The strongest single argument that a name is a filter, not a cause.

### Four lessons for tools

1. Short beats clever: two or three syllables, sayable in English and in the target market's language without spelling. Linear, Clay, Dust, Neon.
2. A displaced real word beats a portmanteau. Cursor explains nothing and everyone understands after a week; "Codepilot Pro AI" explains everything and does not exist.
3. A first name is a strategy, not a cute idea: it humanises a system people are afraid to treat as a person. Fragile when the product is a dashboard.
4. Granola beats "Deepvault": a name slightly beside the point, held by confident design, beats a name that means AI. The market is saturated with meaning and short of character.
