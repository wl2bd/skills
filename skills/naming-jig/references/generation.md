# Generation techniques

Generation is a volume phase with structure. Work territory by territory, technique by technique, and tag candidates by type as you go. The obvious layer of each territory must be produced *and passed through*: it exists in every competitor's brainstorm too, so its value is as a floor, not a ceiling.

## 1. Lexical field mining

For each territory, expand systematically, not associatively:

- **Synonyms and near-synonyms**: then the rarer registers of each (ledger, not just book).
- **Adjacent objects and tools**: what physically lives in this territory? (Time: dial, pendulum, escapement, bell, sundial.)
- **Actions and verbs**: verb-names feel alive and product-like (Stripe, Notion-adjacent "do" words).
- **Places, materials, textures**: concrete beats abstract; a name you can touch is a name you remember.
- **Myth, history, science registers**: figures, phenomena, instruments. Skip the top layer (Apollo, Titan, Phoenix are wallpaper; see anti-patterns) and dig into the second and third rank.

## 2. Cross-language raids

Take the territory's key concepts into other languages: Latin and Greek roots, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, Nordic languages, Swahili, wherever the brief's tone points. Rules:

- Verify actual meaning and connotation with sources in that language; never trust surface beauty.
- Prefer words a target-market speaker can pronounce on first read.
- A lightly *adapted* foreign word (trimmed, respelled for the target market) often beats the raw import.

## 3. Morpheme construction (coined names)

Build, don't shake syllables:

- Choose 1-2 **meaningful roots** from the territories (Latin/Greek or modern fragments).
- Assemble with connective vowels; aim for CV-alternating structures (consonant-vowel): they travel across languages best.
- 2-3 syllables; stress lands naturally on the first.
- Then *stress-test the result against the anti-patterns file*: does it smell pharma, crypto, or syllable-shaker? Kill it if so.

## 4. Sound-first construction

Sometimes design the phonetics before the meaning. Sound symbolism is real and usable; Lexicon Branding (the firm behind Swiffer, BlackBerry, Dasani, and Pentium) famously runs linguistic research on it:

- **Plosives** (B, P, T, K, D, G): hard attack, punchy, memorable. Names that open *and* close on plosives (Kodak) have maximum percussive frame.
- **Fricatives and sibilants** (F, S, V, Z): speed, smoothness, air (Swiffer's S+F does the product's job).
- **Liquids and nasals** (L, M, N, R): soft, warm, flowing; good for care, calm, premium.
- **Front vowels** (i, e): read as small, quick, precise, light.
- **Back vowels** (o, u, a): read as large, heavy, powerful, slow.
- **Bouba/kiki**: round sounds map to round/soft meanings, sharp sounds to angular/fast ones, across cultures. Align the sound shape with the brand's shape.

Match the phonetic profile to the brief's tone adjectives, then find or build words that fit the profile.

## 5. Metaphor chains

Walk the chain: *function → effect → feeling → image*. A backup tool: copies files → nothing is ever lost → relief, permanence → amber, bedrock, echo, lighthouse. Name from the far end of the chain, not the near end: the near end is descriptive, the far end is evocative.

## 6. Arbitrary harvesting

Collect real, concrete, textured words with no category link at all (objects, animals, materials, weather) and test them against the brief's tone. This feels wrong and produces some of the strongest candidates on the sheet (see `name-types.md` on arbitrary marks). Selection criteria: sensory texture, pleasant mouthfeel, clean baggage.

## 7. Mechanical variations (last, and lightly)

Truncations (Cisco), reduplications, respellings, letter swaps, applied to *good* candidates from the passes above, never as a primary source. Every respelling must buy something specific (distinctiveness, trademark room) and its cost is permanent (the spelling tax).

## 8. Domain-native construction (domain hacks)

Only when the said-versus-typed rule in `name-types.md` §14 allows it. Start from the phrase, not the word:

- Write 10 to 20 two-word phrases that describe the product or the feeling of using it, then look for a TLD that can play the second word. Working stock, September 2026: .tools, .app, .dev, .page, .site, .menu, .help, .today, .work, .name, .studio, .design, .haus, .club, .golf, .cv, .me, .ng, .ly, .sh, .io, .ai, .so, .to, .is, .it. Check the live list at IANA before proposing a TLD; new gTLDs open and close.
- Two shapes: the TLD completes a phrase (`early.tools`, `pre.page`, `tido.today`) or the TLD finishes a word (`vibi.ng`, `linkedinto.cv`, `bit.ly`). The first is more sayable; the second is more memorable and more fragile.
- The dot must fall where a natural pause falls. If reading it aloud requires saying "dot" in the middle of a word, it fails the radio test (`are.na` and `lu.ma` accepted that tax knowingly; say so if you propose one).
- Hyphenated .com as a cousin: `any-feeds.com`, `feed-letter.com`. Readable, available, but the hyphen leaks into the spoken name and is a known spelling tax. Offer it only when the unhyphenated .com is taken and the name is strong enough to pay it.
- Every domain hack proposed for a tool ships with its fallback on the same line, in case the project outgrows it: `early.tools` (fallback: Early, early.com REGISTERED). The user sees at once what changing scale would cost.

## 9. Series grammars (naming a family, not a name)

Use when the brief says the name is the first of a series (tools, packages, skills, features). The object of the process is then a naming system, and the shortlist has to show the system. Three grammars; name the one you are using, never let it emerge by accident:

1. **Constant left, variable right.** `early.tools`, `early.domains`. The word is the brand, the TLD names the category. Most legible: the family reads as a family on first sight. Most fragile: every future tool needs a TLD that exists and reads well, and the brand word must stretch to categories not yet imagined.
2. **Variable left, constant right.** `tasteful.haus`, `belong.haus`; `read.cv`, `posts.cv`; `something-forge`, `other-forge`. The suffix is the brand, each tool gets its own word. Most flexible: any future tool fits as long as a word can be found. Cost: the suffix carries the brand alone, so it must be distinctive on its own (`-haus` is; `-app` is not).
3. **Recombined pivot word.** `any-feeds`, `feed-letter`. One word reappears in a different position. Weakest: reads as a series only to someone who already knows both members. Use it only when the pivot word is the product's whole identity.

Rules for the axis:

- The constant part carries the brand; the variable part carries the category or the job. Never the reverse, or the second name will look like a competitor of the first.
- In series mode the type quotas of step 3 apply to the constant, not to each compound: every member is a compound by construction. A coined constant pays its explanation tax on every member and in every language; prefer a real word.
- The kill list also runs on the family: a constant that reads as a different product next to one plausible sibling (`-press` next to `readme`, `-drill` next to `french`) fails the neighbor test before it fails anything else.
- The constant must survive the third and fourth member. Test it against two categories the user has not planned yet.
- Domain hacks and series go together naturally: the TLD is either the variable (grammar 1) or the constant (grammar 2). This is the strongest argument for the domain-native type when the brief names a tool.
- A series without an axis is a list. Google's generative family (Gemini, Astra, Aura, Jules, Lyria, Veo, Flow, Imagen, plus Bard before it) has nine names and no grammar: no constant part, no shared TLD, no shared type. Compare Anthropic (Claude, then Opus / Sonnet / Haiku: one first name, one poetic-form axis) and OpenAI (GPT-n plus the o-series: descriptive, plain, but systematic).
- For a company, do not build a series. Build an umbrella brand and put descriptive names under it (`Brand Studio`, `Brand Cloud`); the umbrella carries the trademark, the descriptors are free to change. Series grammars are for portfolios of tools, not for corporate architecture.

---

**Hygiene during generation:**
- Keep the raw sheet organized by territory and tagged by type; it feeds the quota check.
- Don't self-censor for availability yet; verification comes after screening.
- Do self-censor for pronounceability: if a target-market reader stumbles, it's dead later anyway.
- When a territory keeps producing wallpaper, the territory is probably too generic: go back and sharpen it rather than pushing harder.
